After three relentless years of wage inflation that has reshaped every hospitality business in the UK, something unexpected has happened.

Average weekly earnings in accommodation and food services fell 1.4% in August 2026 — from £354 to £349 — according to the latest ONS labour market figures. It is the first annual decline in hospitality wages in over 30 months.

Vacancies have fallen too. The sector had 70,000 open roles in the three months to September 2026, down 7% year-on-year.

For franchisees who have spent the past three years absorbing wage increase after wage increase — 69.6% since 2016, two significant NLW jumps in consecutive Aprils, employer NI rising to 15% — this looks like good news.

It is more complicated than that.

## Why the Number Is Smaller Than It Looks

A 1.4% fall in average weekly earnings sounds meaningful. In the context of what has happened to hospitality wages over the past three years, it is a rounding error.

The National Living Wage rose from £11.44 in April 2024 to £12.21 in April 2025. It rose again to £12.71 in April 2026. Those increases — compounding on top of the employer NI change that came in simultaneously last April — added tens of thousands of pounds to the annual payroll of any unit employing more than a handful of staff.

A 1.4% dip does not reverse that. It does not come close.

What it suggests, as RSM UK partner Saxon Moseley puts it, is that the sector may have reached "an equilibrium" — a point where wages have stopped climbing at the pace of the past three years. That is not the same as costs coming down. It is the rate of increase slowing.

For a franchisee running a unit where labour represents 32–35% of revenue, the difference between wages rising 8% per year and wages being flat matters. It does not transform the unit economics, but it removes one of the escalating pressures that has been grinding at margins since 2022.

## The Part Nobody Is Celebrating

The vacancy fall tells a different story to the wage fall — and it is the story that deserves more attention.

Hospitality has lost approximately 100,000 jobs over the past two years, according to UKHospitality analysis of ONS data. That is 45% of all jobs lost across the entire UK economy in the same period.

A sector that accounts for 31% of first jobs for people aged 16 to 24 has been shrinking its headcount at a pace that nobody should be comfortable with.

As Saxon Moseley notes: "Another fall in hospitality vacancies partly points to operators being leaner with their operations and creating efficiencies through AI, but more worryingly, it also highlights businesses are being priced out of hiring."

Being priced out of hiring is not a margin improvement story. It is a capacity story. Operators cutting hours, reducing covers, running leaner teams, and doing more with less are not thriving — they are coping. Some are coping well. Others are one difficult quarter away from not coping at all.

The wage equilibrium is partly a function of fewer jobs being created and fewer vacancies being filled. A market that has stopped bidding wages up because it has stopped expanding is not the same as a market that has found sustainable stability.

## What This Means for Anyone Evaluating a Franchise Right Now

**Labour cost assumptions need to be built from today's actuals, not from trend projections.** For two years, the sensible approach was to model wage costs rising 6–9% per year. The August data suggests that trajectory may have plateaued, at least temporarily. But the April 2027 NLW announcement is imminent, and the sector is bracing for another increase. A unit economics model built around sustained 8% annual wage inflation may be slightly more conservative than current data warrants — but flat wages are not yet confirmed.

**Staffing structure is a competitive advantage right now.** The brands and franchise concepts that have invested in technology-led labour scheduling — AI-driven shift planning, demand forecasting, smarter rostering — are running leaner teams more efficiently than those relying on traditional staffing models. The franchisees doing best are the ones who have built operations that are not dependent on filling every role, every shift, at whatever rate the market demands.

**The Budget will reset the picture.** Whatever the August wage data shows, it will be substantially modified by what Chancellor John Healey announces on 28 October 2026. The April 2027 NLW rate will be confirmed. The sector is lobbying hard for a NI reduction and a freeze on minimum wage increases. If the Budget delivers on any of those asks, the labour cost outlook for 2027 looks materially different. If it doesn't, the equilibrium in the August data will be short-lived.

## The Bottom Line

Every franchisee, every prospective franchisee, and every franchisor in the UK hospitality sector should have one eye on the Budget announcement. The decisions made will shape the labour cost structure of hospitality operations through 2027 and beyond.

The wage equilibrium is real. It is also fragile. And it may not survive the Budget.
