The wave of American hospitality brands heading for the UK is impossible to ignore. Chili's, Jersey Mike's Subs, Coyo Taco, Raising Cane's, Carl's Jr, and Chick-fil-A are all establishing a presence here. For franchisees, this raises an obvious question: are these exciting new opportunities, or untested propositions in a market these brands don't yet understand?

The honest answer is that they can be either. Which one depends entirely on what you look at before you decide.

## Why US brands are coming now

From the franchisor's perspective, the timing makes perfect sense. The UK is the world's fifth largest economy with a mature food and beverage culture and established franchise infrastructure. For a US brand with 500 or 1,000 units at home, the UK looks like white space.

Finding a master franchisee or development partner to take on the capital and operational risk is an efficient way to extend international footprint without proportional investment. This is rational behaviour from the franchisor's side — but it does not, by itself, tell you whether the opportunity is right for a UK franchisee in 2026.

## What US brands get right — and what they don't

American brands that have cracked the UK market — McDonald's, KFC, Subway, Five Guys, Shake Shack, and Wingstop — share a critical common factor: operational rigour. Their training programmes, supply chain infrastructure, and franchisee support systems were built for scale and tested across thousands of units before they arrived in the UK.

The brands arriving now are at a very different stage. A concept entering the UK for the first time, however established in the US or internationally, has:

- Zero UK trading history
- No existing UK franchisees you can speak with
- No UK-specific unit economics data
- No evidence of performance in the current UK cost environment

This is not a disqualifying problem. It is a due diligence problem.

## The format question matters more than the brand name

US brands entering the UK in 2026 are overwhelmingly concentrated in fried chicken, burgers, fast-casual sandwiches, and quick-service restaurants. This is not coincidental — these are the formats that have performed most strongly in the UK market over the past three years.

Chili's, as a casual dining brand, is an outlier. Casual dining is the segment that has struggled most in the UK — high-street restaurant brands declined 26% in 2024 whilst QSR grew 10%. The case for a casual dining brand from the US requires more scrutiny than a fried chicken or fast-casual concept, because the format faces structural headwinds that the brand's US performance does not reflect.

The format is as important as the brand. Signing with a well-known name in a format that is structurally under pressure in the UK carries a different risk profile to signing with a less recognised name in a format that is growing.

## What you cannot get — and what you can

You cannot get UK-specific performance data from a brand new to the market. You cannot speak with existing UK franchisees or review three years of trading accounts. You cannot verify what unit economics look like in a UK cost environment, because that data does not yet exist.

What you can get is the brand's performance data from comparable international markets. Not the US — the cost structure, consumer behaviour, and regulatory environment are too different. But established markets with relevant characteristics to the UK: Australia, Canada, parts of Europe.

If a brand has franchisees in Germany or the Netherlands trading profitably at current European wage levels, that is more useful context than US performance figures.

You can also interrogate the franchisor's UK business plan in detail:

- How have they modelled the UK cost environment — specifically the National Living Wage trajectory, employer NI, and business rates?
- Have they built a unit economics model from UK cost benchmarks, or translated US assumptions with a currency adjustment?
- Can they produce a model built from UK cost data?

If the franchisor cannot answer these questions, you are being asked to take a significant risk on incomplete information.

## The master franchisee structure adds complexity

Several US brands entering the UK are doing so via master franchise agreements — where a single UK operator takes on development rights and sub-franchises individual units. This means you are not in a direct relationship with the US brand. You are in a relationship with the master franchisee.

The support you receive, the unit economics model you are given, and your ongoing relationship all flow through that intermediary. The quality of the master franchisee's operation matters as much as the quality of the underlying brand.

When evaluating a US brand through a master franchisee, ask:

- How long have they been operating in the UK?
- What is their track record in hospitality?
- What does their franchisee support infrastructure look like?

A master franchisee with limited hospitality experience and no established UK network is a significant risk factor, regardless of how strong the underlying US brand is.

## The opportunity is real — with the right preparation

This is not an argument against US brands entering the UK, or against evaluating them as franchise opportunities. The UK's QSR and fast-casual market is growing, and a well-executed US concept with genuine consumer appeal can perform very strongly.

But "well-known American brand" is not, by itself, due diligence. A brand with 1,600 US restaurants and no UK track record requires the same rigour as any other opportunity you would evaluate — arguably more, because you are being asked to proceed without the most important data point: how this specific concept performs in the UK market under current UK cost conditions.

The franchisees who do well with new market entrants are the ones who go in with realistic, UK-grounded unit economics, a clear-eyed view of the format's position in the UK market, and proper understanding of the master franchisee or development partner they are working with.

The franchisees who struggle are the ones who were sold on the brand name and did not look hard enough at the numbers.
