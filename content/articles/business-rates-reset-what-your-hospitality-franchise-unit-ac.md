## What changed on 31 March 2026

The temporary business rates relief scheme that supported UK hospitality through the pandemic has ended. For years, qualifying properties received 40% off their rates bill, capped at £110,000 per business.

It has been replaced with a permanent but structurally different system: permanently lower business rates multipliers specifically for retail, hospitality and leisure (RHL) properties.

## The new multiplier structure

From April 2026, the rates you pay depend on your property's rateable value:

- **Small business RHL multiplier** (rateable value under £51,000): 38.2p in the pound
- **Standard RHL multiplier** (rateable value £51,000–£499,999): 43p in the pound
- **High-value multiplier** (rateable value £500,000+): 50.8p in the pound

For comparison, non-RHL properties pay 43.2p and 48p respectively. Properties below £500,000 rateable value are now 5p below the standard rate—permanently, not year-on-year.

The government describes this as a £1 billion annual tax cut benefitting 750,000+ properties. That's true in aggregate. It is not true for every individual operator.

## Why your bill may have moved in either direction

The old relief scheme had two key features: it was a percentage discount, and it came with a £110,000 cash cap per business. Multi-site operators could only claim relief on the first £110,000 across their entire portfolio.

The new multiplier system has no cash cap. Every qualifying property benefits in full.

**For large multi-unit franchisees**, this could mean a meaningful improvement. **For single-site operators**, the picture is more complex.

On top of the multiplier change, 2026 brought a full revaluation of non-domestic property rateable values—the first since 2023. If your property's rateable value has risen, you are calculating your bill on a higher base number, even with the lower multiplier. If it has fallen, you may benefit more than expected.

The government introduced a Supporting Small Business scheme to smooth sharp increases for properties losing RHL relief. Pubs and live music venues have an additional 15% relief plus a two-year bill freeze in real terms.

**Your actual rates liability for 2026/27 depends on at least four variables:**

- Your new rateable value
- Which multiplier you qualify for
- Whether you are receiving transitional or Supporting Small Business relief
- Whether you are a pub entitled to additional discount

You cannot know your liability without checking your bill.

## What this means for unit economics

Business rates typically represent 8–15% of revenue for a hospitality unit. For a lean QSR or café, they are manageable. For a restaurant with a larger footprint in a high-value location, they sit alongside rent and labour as one of the heaviest line items on the P&L.

Crucially, rates are fixed regardless of performance. A 5–6% royalty falls in quiet months. Your rates bill does not. If your 2026/27 bill has increased, that cost sits on your P&L every month whether the unit trades well or not.

For **prospective franchisees currently in due diligence**, this is critical. Many franchise financial projections prepared before April 2026 do not reflect the correct rates liability. That is not necessarily the franchisor's fault—the revaluation and multiplier changes landed together—but an outdated rates figure in a unit economics model can be the difference between a projection that works and one that does not.

**The question to ask is simple: what does the rates bill look like for this specific site under the April 2026 multipliers and the new rateable value?** If the franchisor cannot answer with a number—not an estimate, a number—get the answer independently before signing anything.

## The wider cost picture

The rates change has landed in the same quarter as other significant cost increases:

- **National Living Wage** increased to £12.71 per hour in April 2026 (4% rise, following a 9.8% increase in April 2025)
- **Employer National Insurance** is now 15%, up from 13.8% before April 2025

For a hospitality unit employing 15 people at or near the NLW, the cumulative effect of two years of increases represents a significant shift in labour costs.

The combined effect of rates restructure, NLW increase, and NI increase means the cost base of a hospitality franchise unit in 2026 looks materially different to one modelled in 2024 or early 2025.

This is not an argument against franchising. It is an argument for ensuring unit economics have been built from current figures, not last year's. Businesses that struggle are often not those with weak brands or poor locations—they are those that signed agreements based on projections that did not account for where costs would be in year two and year three.

## If you are already operating

Three things are worth doing now:

**Check your new rateable value.** The Valuation Office Agency's online tool allows you to look up your 2026 assessment. If you believe it is too high, a formal challenge is possible, though the window for checking underlying data is time-sensitive. Engaging a rating agent to review it is worth the cost if your rateable value is significant.

**Confirm the correct multiplier has been applied.** Your local authority should have applied the right RHL multiplier automatically, but errors occur. Check your 2026/27 bill against the multiplier that should apply to your property type and rateable value.

**Check for transitional relief.** If your bill has increased sharply, the Supporting Small Business scheme should have been applied automatically. If you are a pub operator, the additional 15% relief and two-year freeze should also be on your bill. If they are not, contact your billing authority.

## If you are evaluating a franchise

Ask for the rates figure as a line item in the unit economics model. Ask specifically whether it has been updated to reflect the 2026/27 multipliers and the new rateable value for the site in question. If the figure is based on the 2025/26 relief scheme, it needs to be recalculated.

If you are looking at a site that has not yet been identified, build the rates assessment into your site evaluation process before you commit to a location. Rateable values and resulting liability can vary significantly between two units in the same town, let alone across regions.

This is exactly the kind of detail that gets missed when a prospective franchisee is focused on the headline opportunity, the brand, the territory, and the revenue projections, whilst losing sight of the fixed cost base.

Go in with your eyes open, with a model that assumes a realistic cost base, not the best case.
