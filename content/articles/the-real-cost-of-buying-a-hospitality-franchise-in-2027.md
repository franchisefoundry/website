A franchise investment involves far more than the initial franchise fee. With operating costs continuing to challenge the UK hospitality sector, prospective franchisees need to understand the full financial commitment before taking the plunge.

Buying a franchise can be an attractive route into business ownership. You gain access to an established brand, an existing operating model and, in many cases, training and ongoing support from an experienced franchisor. But one of the biggest mistakes prospective franchisees make is focusing too heavily on the headline investment figure.

A franchise advertised as requiring £100,000 to get started may involve considerably more once property costs, equipment, staffing, working capital and ongoing fees are taken into account. With the Autumn Budget introducing further changes to business rates and employment costs, understanding the numbers has never been more important.

## Look Beyond the Initial Franchise Fee

The franchise fee is often the first figure investors notice. However, it is only one part of the overall investment. Depending on the business model, the initial investment could include:

- Initial franchise fee
- Property deposit and legal costs
- Design, planning and professional fees
- Building works and fit-out
- Equipment and technology
- Initial stock and supplies
- Recruitment and staff training
- Marketing and launch costs
- Working capital

For a food or hospitality franchise, property and fit-out costs can represent a substantial proportion of the total investment. It is important to establish exactly what is included in the franchisor's advertised investment figure, what is excluded and whether the estimate is based on a realistic opening scenario.

## Understand the Impact of Business Rates

Business rates remain an important consideration for anyone looking to open a physical hospitality business. The Autumn Budget 2026 announced changes that will provide additional support to qualifying retail, hospitality and leisure businesses in England from April 2027:

- 100% relief will apply up to a rateable value of £18,000 (increasing from £12,000)
- Tapered relief will apply up to £21,000 (increasing from £15,000)
- Qualifying retail, hospitality and leisure properties will benefit from a two-year real-terms freeze on business rates bills in 2027/28 and 2028/29

The Government estimates that around 70,000 additional businesses will pay no business rates as a result. This is welcome news, but investors should not assume that every hospitality franchise will qualify for full relief. Eligibility depends on the property, its rateable value and the applicable rules. Before committing to a site, understand its rateable value and obtain a realistic estimate of the business rates payable.

## Staffing Costs Need to Be Built Into the Model

Labour is one of the largest operating costs for many hospitality businesses. The National Living Wage will increase to £13.21 per hour from April 2027—a 3.9% increase. For a business employing several full-time and part-time team members, even relatively small hourly increases can have a meaningful impact on annual costs.

Investors should look beyond the hourly wage and consider the wider employment costs, including:

- Employer National Insurance
- Pension contributions
- Recruitment and training
- Holiday cover
- Staff turnover

It is also worth understanding how dependent the business is on staffing levels. Can the operation run efficiently with a lean team, or does it require a large workforce to deliver the customer experience? A strong franchise model should have a clear and realistic staffing structure.

## Don't Overlook Ongoing Franchise Fees

Once the business is open, the financial commitment doesn't stop. Most franchise agreements include ongoing fees, which may include:

- Royalty or management fees
- Marketing and brand contributions
- Technology or software fees
- Required supplier or purchasing arrangements
- Other operational or administrative charges

These fees may be calculated as a percentage of turnover, a fixed monthly amount or a combination of both. It is important to understand how they are calculated and how they affect profitability. A royalty calculated on turnover is generally payable regardless of whether the business makes a profit in a particular month.

## Working Capital Can Make or Break a New Franchise

One of the most overlooked parts of a franchise investment is working capital. Opening a business is one challenge. Having enough money to operate it while it establishes itself is another.

A new franchise may take time to build its customer base, reach expected sales levels and develop consistent cash flow. During this period, the business still needs to pay rent, wages, suppliers, utilities and franchise fees. Investors should understand:

- How much working capital is required
- How long it may need to last
- Whether they have sufficient funds available if trading takes longer than expected

It is also sensible to model a slower-than-expected opening period rather than relying entirely on the franchisor's target performance.

## Stress-Test the Financial Projections

A financial model should not simply show what happens if everything goes to plan. Prospective franchisees should ask what happens if:

- Sales are 15% lower than forecast
- Staffing costs increase
- The opening date is delayed
- Fit-out costs exceed the original budget
- Rent or utilities are higher than expected
- The business takes longer to reach break-even

These scenarios help investors understand how much financial resilience they need. It is also important to distinguish between turnover, operating profit, cash flow and the amount the owner can actually take out of the business. A business can generate impressive revenue figures whilst still producing relatively modest returns for its owner.

## The Bottom Line

Franchising can provide a structured route into business ownership, but an established brand does not remove the financial risks of running a business. The latest Budget provides some welcome support for parts of the hospitality sector, but investors still need to account for wages, property costs, ongoing fees and the realities of operating a business.

The most important question isn't simply "how much does it cost to buy the franchise?" It's whether the business can generate a sustainable return after all the costs have been accounted for.
