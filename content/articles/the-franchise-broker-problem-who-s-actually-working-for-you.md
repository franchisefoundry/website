## The uncomfortable truth about franchise brokers

Here's something most prospective franchisees don't know before they start looking: the broker guiding them through the process is paid by the franchisor, not by you. They receive 30–50% of the franchise fee when you sign. The service to you is presented as free.

These two facts aren't unrelated. They describe a structural conflict of interest baked into the traditional franchise broker model — one that shapes how the guidance you receive is actually delivered.

## How franchise brokers are paid

The mechanics are straightforward. A franchise brand wants to grow its network. Rather than running its own recruitment operation, it works with brokers who maintain databases of prospective franchisees and make introductions on a success-fee basis.

When you sign, the broker receives a commission from the franchisor — typically 30–50% of the initial franchise fee.

- On a £25,000 franchise fee: £7,500–£12,500 per placed franchisee
- On higher-fee brands: proportionally larger commissions

The financial incentive is entirely structured around completions. There is no payment for:

- Slowing the process down
- Identifying that a candidate isn't financially prepared
- Recommending that a particular brand isn't right for a particular person

## Where the incentives lead in practice

This isn't an attack on individual brokers — there are many who work with genuine integrity. The point is that the structural incentive of the model doesn't reward careful assessment. It penalises it financially.

### Speed over rigour

A thorough assessment of your financial position, operational readiness, and suitability for a specific brand takes time. Time spent on diligence that ends in "not yet" generates no return. The incentive is to move quickly and present the process as matching rather than filtering.

### Volume over quality

A broker representing 50 or 100 brands has a financial incentive to find *a* brand that fits rather than to evaluate whether *any* of them is genuinely the right choice. Franchise portals are paid per click — they have no structural incentive to curate quality.

### Optimistic framing

A broker presenting a brand has no incentive to lead with uncomfortable data: bottom-quartile unit economics, franchisees who struggled in year two, network exit rates. None of this requires bad faith. It simply follows where the financial incentive points.

## What the data shows

The consequences are visible in franchise performance research:

- **80%** of brands that enter franchising stop within five years. Poor franchisee selection is the primary cause — not bad concepts or markets, but the wrong people in unsuitable units.
- **71%** of prospective franchisees disengage during recruitment. In 87.5% of cases, the reason is communication failure.
- A recruitment process designed for completions rather than fit produces high attrition and candidates motivated by urgency rather than suitability.
- **Misrepresentation** around earnings projections and financial performance is the most common ground for franchise legal disputes in the UK.
- **1 in 5** new franchisees rate their training and support as poor or average.

## The regulation gap

Franchise brokers in the UK are not regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority. There is no mandatory qualification. There is no required disclosure of commission structures to prospective franchisees.

The BFA has a voluntary code of conduct, but membership is optional and compliance is self-reported.

This means that relying on a traditional broker for guidance on a £200,000 investment decision gives you fewer protections than someone buying a £500 financial product through a regulated adviser.

## Protect yourself: the questions to ask

You can't entirely remove the structural conflict of interest from the broker relationship. What you can do is ask the right questions to understand whose interests are being served:

**How many brands does this broker represent?**
A broker with 50 or 100 brands has a different incentive structure to one with 10 or 15 carefully selected opportunities. Volume and quality tend to be inversely correlated.

**How is the broker paid, and by whom?**
If they cannot or won't answer directly, that's informative. Commission-based compensation by the franchisor is standard — but it should be disclosed.

**What brands has the broker turned down, and why?**
A broker who has never turned down a brand has never applied meaningful criteria. Ask what they look for and what they've said no to.

**What does the assessment process look like?**
If the process feels designed to move you toward a signing rather than to test whether you're ready for one, pay attention.

**Can you speak with franchisees who didn't proceed?**
Not just the success stories. The candidates who went through the process and decided not to sign are often the most revealing data points about whether a broker's guidance is genuinely in your interest.

## The bottom line

The franchise sector in the UK is growing and contains genuine opportunities. Navigating it well requires understanding not just the opportunity itself, but the incentives of the people presenting it to you. That understanding is the most protective thing a prospective franchisee can have.
