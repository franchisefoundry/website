Most prospective franchisees approach franchise investment by starting with a brand they recognise or like. The format tends to be assumed rather than chosen. That is the wrong order.

The format determines your cost structure, your revenue model, your staffing requirements, your exposure to delivery platforms, and your likely margin under current UK conditions. Getting the format right first makes the brand decision significantly clearer.

This matters now more than ever. Last week, accountancy group UHY Hacker Young published data showing that profits at the UK's 100 largest restaurant groups fell by 44% in the latest year—despite combined turnover rising from £12.9 billion to £13.3 billion. Revenue went up. Profit went down. The gap was absorbed by rising employment costs, higher business rates, and continued food and energy inflation.

That is not a crisis story for UK hospitality franchising. It is a margin story. And it is the most useful lens through which to choose the format you invest in.

## QSR — the growth story with a labour caveat

Quick service restaurants are the strongest-performing format in UK hospitality right now by volume and growth rate. The UK QSR market is valued at approximately £35.77 billion in 2026 and growing at a 5.23% compound annual rate. In 2024, QSR outlets grew 10% while high-street restaurant brands declined 26%.

**Why it works in 2026**

QSR's cost structure suits the current environment better than full-service formats:

- Labour ratios are lower relative to revenue
- The format is built for speed and throughput
- Consumers cutting back on eating out choose QSR because it feels like affordable spending, not a sacrifice
- Brands with strong loyalty apps and owned delivery channels are capturing direct revenue that avoids the 15–30% delivery platform commission entirely

**What to watch**

Labour is still the biggest cost line, typically 30–35% of revenue, and the National Living Wage trajectory is a persistent headwind regardless of format. The combined food and labour prime cost needs to sit below 65% of revenue for a QSR unit to be viable. Some franchisees are running above that threshold, particularly in London and other high-wage urban markets.

**Investment range**

- Entry-level QSR: £100,000–£200,000
- Mid-market QSR: £250,000–£500,000
- Premium QSR (major branded chains): £800,000–£2.2 million

**Best suited to**

Operators comfortable with high-volume, fast-paced environments and people who can manage teams efficiently and build systems-led operations. First-time franchisees at the entry-level end; experienced multi-unit operators at the premium end.

## Coffee and cafés — accessible entry, compressed margins

The UK branded coffee shop market is worth £6.1 billion and growing at approximately 4.1% annually. As a franchise format, coffee is appealing for obvious reasons: relatively accessible investment levels, high footfall frequency, and strong brand loyalty among regular customers.

**Why it works in 2026**

Coffee is a low-ticket, high-frequency purchase that holds up well in cost-of-living pressure environments. People who are cutting back on restaurant visits are less likely to give up their morning coffee. Mobile and kiosk formats at the lower end of the investment range can generate positive returns quickly with the right location.

**What to watch**

The margin structure in coffee is under pressure from two directions simultaneously. Coffee bean prices have been volatile due to climate-related supply disruption. The market is also crowded, with strong independent operators competing on quality at a level that branded chains find difficult to match in every location.

The difference between a well-located and a poorly-located coffee franchise is larger than in most formats. Site selection is everything.

**Investment range**

- Mobile units: from around £50,000
- Kiosk formats: £80,000–£150,000
- Full café format with an established brand: £200,000–£350,000

**Best suited to**

Operators who genuinely care about the product and the customer experience. People comfortable running lean operations with small teams. Good for first-time franchisees with lower capital. Requires strong location identification skills—the site decision is more consequential in coffee than in almost any other format.

## Pubs — the most complex format, with genuine opportunity

The UK pub estate has contracted from approximately 60,800 venues in 2000 to around 45,000 today. Within the managed and franchised pub sector, however, the picture is more positive—managed operators are projected to reach over 50% of total sector revenue by 2027.

**Why it works in 2026**

The right pub in the right location, with the right operator, generates a loyal local customer base highly resistant to consumer behaviour shifts. Managed franchise models offer relatively low entry costs with guaranteed income structures that reduce early-stage financial risk.

**What to watch**

The Beer Tie remains significant complexity for anyone entering a tied tenancy. Drinks purchased through the pub company's supply arrangement typically carry above-market prices—compressing the margin on your biggest revenue line. Anyone entering the tied pub sector without a proper understanding of the Beer Tie and its unit economics implications is taking on risk they may not have quantified.

Wet-led pubs are more exposed to consumer spending pressure than food-led managed pubs.

**Investment range**

- Managed franchise models (ingoing only): £3,000–£15,000 with guaranteed income
- Traditional tenancies and leaseholds: £15,000–£100,000+
- Freehold operations: £500,000–£1.5 million+

**Best suited to**

Operators with genuine community instinct and relationship-building skills. People who understand or are willing to learn the complexity of the Beer Tie. Experienced hospitality operators at the tenancy and leasehold level.

## Casual dining — the most challenging format in 2026

This is where the UHY Hacker Young data published this week bites hardest. The profit collapse at the UK's top 100 restaurant groups—44% fall in profit despite rising turnover—is most acute in the casual dining segment. High-street restaurant brands declined 26% in 2024.

The format is carrying the heaviest cost burden (full kitchen brigade, front-of-house team, larger footprint, higher rent) against a consumer base increasingly reluctant to spend £50–£70 on a mid-market sit-down meal.

**Where the opportunity still exists**

There are well-run casual dining franchise concepts with genuine consumer propositions performing well in 2026—particularly those that have differentiated on experience, on a specific cuisine, or on a value proposition that holds up under current spending pressure. Brands that have shifted menus toward higher-margin dishes, embraced smaller dish formats, and invested in loyalty infrastructure are in better shape than the sector average.

**What to watch**

The cost structure is the most demanding of any format. Food and labour combined regularly exceed 65% of revenue at casual dining units. Rent and occupancy costs are typically higher than QSR because the format requires more space.

Going into a casual dining franchise with an optimistic revenue assumption that does not materialise in year one can quickly produce a unit that is very hard to turn around.

**Investment range**

£200,000–£800,000 depending on format, footprint, and brand. Fit-out costs are typically higher than QSR due to the dining environment requirements.

**Best suited to**

Experienced hospitality operators with a strong understanding of the full-service restaurant P&L. People with genuine passion for a specific cuisine or dining concept. Operators with a clear, well-researched location thesis—the difference between a profitable and an unprofitable casual dining unit is very often the site decision.

## How to use this framework

The point of choosing format before brand is that it removes a layer of noise from the decision.

If you have assessed your own profile honestly—your capital, your experience, your appetite for complexity, your preferred working style—against the four formats above, the universe of appropriate brands narrows considerably. You are no longer evaluating every franchise that appears on a portal. You are evaluating the brands in the format that suits you, using unit economics benchmarks that are specific to that format.

The questions that matter at the brand level then become clearer:

- Does this brand's unit economics model meet the format benchmarks—prime cost below 65%, net margin after royalties and rent hitting at least 8–10%, payback within five to six years at realistic trading assumptions?
- Does the brand's support infrastructure match the complexity of the format?
- Does the brand have a UK track record under current cost conditions?

**Format first. Brand second. Numbers throughout. That is the sequence that produces good franchise decisions.**
