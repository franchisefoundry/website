A franchise turning over £1 million might sound like an attractive investment. But here's the crucial question: how much of that revenue actually makes its way to the bottom line?

When researching franchise opportunities, it's natural to be drawn towards impressive sales figures. A franchisor might highlight a location generating £750,000 in annual turnover, or a successful franchisee operating several sites. These figures can help demonstrate the potential scale of a business, but they don't tell the whole story.

## Turnover is only the starting point

Turnover is the total value of sales generated by a business over a particular period. A restaurant generating £800,000 in annual sales looks impressive on paper. But that £800,000 needs to cover all the costs involved in running the operation:

- Food and drink ingredients
- Packaging and consumables
- Staff wages and employment costs
- Rent and business rates
- Utilities
- Insurance
- Marketing
- Repairs and maintenance
- Franchise royalties
- Technology and software
- Other operating expenses

Once these costs are deducted, the amount remaining can look very different from the original turnover figure. This is why investors should never assess a franchise based on sales alone.

## Understand gross profit and gross margin

Gross profit is calculated by taking revenue and deducting the direct cost of goods or services sold. For a food business, this typically includes the cost of ingredients and packaging.

Gross margin expresses that gross profit as a percentage of revenue. For example:

- A business generates £100,000 in sales
- Direct product costs are £30,000
- Gross profit is £70,000
- Gross margin is 70%

This metric helps investors understand how efficiently a business generates profit from its sales before covering wider operating expenses. However, a strong gross margin doesn't automatically mean a business is profitable. Staffing, rent and other overheads still need to be covered.

## Operating costs determine what is left

After gross profit comes the cost of running the business. For hospitality franchises, staffing and property costs can have a particularly significant impact.

Consider this illustrative example:

| Financial item | Annual amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | £800,000 |
| Direct product costs | £240,000 |
| Gross profit | £560,000 |
| Staffing costs | £220,000 |
| Rent and business rates | £90,000 |
| Utilities and insurance | £35,000 |
| Marketing and other overheads | £55,000 |
| Franchise and technology fees | £40,000 |
| **Operating profit** | **£120,000** |

In this simplified example, the business generates £800,000 in revenue but produces £120,000 in operating profit—representing an operating margin of just 15%. Two franchises generating the same turnover can produce very different financial results depending on their cost structures.

## Profit isn't the same as cash flow

Another critical distinction: profit and cash flow are not the same thing. A business can be profitable on paper but still experience cash flow difficulties.

For example, it may need to:

- Pay suppliers before receiving money from customers
- Fund stock purchases
- Cover seasonal fluctuations
- Make loan repayments
- Invest in equipment, repairs or additional working capital

Understand these cash flow considerations:

- How much cash does the business need to operate each month?
- Are suppliers paid immediately or on credit terms?
- How does seasonal trading affect cash flow?
- What level of working capital is required?
- Will additional investment be needed after opening?

Cash flow is particularly important during the first year of a new franchise, when sales may take time to reach forecast levels.

## Understand the difference between business profit and owner income

A common mistake is assuming that the profit generated by a franchise is the amount you can take home. This isn't necessarily the case.

The owner may need to:

- Pay themselves a salary for managing the business
- Cover loan repayments
- Meet tax liabilities
- Fund future investment requirements

If you're actively working in the business, it's essential that financial projections include a realistic cost for that role. Otherwise, the apparent profit may partly reflect the fact that you're effectively working without being paid a market-rate salary.

For semi-managed or multi-unit models, this becomes even more important. The cost of employing a manager or management team needs to be reflected in the financial model.

## Ask for evidence behind the financial projections

A franchisor may provide financial projections during the discovery process. These can be useful, but you should understand where the assumptions come from. Ask:

- Are the figures based on actual trading locations or forecasts?
- How many sites have achieved these results?
- What is the average performance across the network?
- How long does it typically take to reach forecast sales?
- Are the figures based on mature locations?
- Do the projections include a realistic owner or manager salary?
- Are franchise fees, rent, rates and staffing included?
- What happens if sales are 10–20% below forecast?

It's also worth speaking with existing franchisees and understanding how their experience compares with the projections. One successful location doesn't necessarily represent typical network performance.

## Look at the return on investment

Ultimately, you need to understand what you're getting back in return for the money you commit. This means looking beyond turnover and even beyond operating profit.

Consider:

- The total initial investment
- Ongoing capital requirements
- The amount of cash the business can generate
- The time it may take to recover your original investment
- The risks involved
- Whether the expected return adequately compensates you for the time, effort and capital committed

A franchise with a lower headline investment isn't automatically better value. Equally, a larger franchise isn't necessarily a better investment. The financial model needs to make sense in the context of your objectives.

## The bottom line

A franchise's turnover might attract your attention, but its underlying financial performance should determine whether you take the opportunity seriously.

Understand gross margins, operating costs, cash flow, owner remuneration and the total investment required. Ask difficult questions and challenge assumptions. A credible franchisor should be able to explain how its financial model works and what sits behind the projections.

Don't invest in a turnover figure. Invest in a business model you understand.
